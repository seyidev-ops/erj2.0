Myth: You Need 5+ Years of Experience to Land a Remote Role

Hundreds of people have told me they'd apply, but "they all want 5 years of experience." Let me show you what's actually happening when you look at the real listings.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 26 April 2026 5 min read

The Myth That's Stopping Thousands of Applications

"I'd love to apply for remote roles, but they all want five years of experience."

I've heard this from hundreds of people. It is the single most common reason people don't apply for the roles they want. And it is based on a misreading of one word.

What the Real Listings Actually Say

I spent two hours on job boards looking for remote entry-level roles posted in the last 30 days. Here's a direct sample of what I found:

  • Junior Copywriter (Remote) — "1 year experience preferred, not required"
  • Remote Data Entry Coordinator — "No prior experience necessary, training provided"
  • Entry-Level Remote Customer Success — "We care more about attitude than resume"
  • Junior UX Researcher (Remote) — "Portfolio required, years of experience not"
  • Remote SDR (Sales) — "Fresh graduates encouraged to apply"

None of these were obscure roles. All were posted within the last 30 days on mainstream platforms.

The Word Everyone Misreads

The most underread word in job descriptions: preferred. Companies write it as a wish list. Most candidates read it as a wall.

When a job description says "5 years preferred" it means: we would ideally like this, but we will hire someone with three years and a portfolio that demonstrates the equivalent capability, or even one year and the right attitude.

The candidates who apply anyway are the ones who get the interview.

What Entry-Level Remote Work Actually Requires

The real barrier to entry-level remote work is not experience. It is positioning.

  • A candidate with 0 years and a strong portfolio beats a candidate with 3 years and a generic CV — every single time
  • A candidate who demonstrates async communication skills and tool proficiency stands out immediately from those who don't mention it
  • A candidate who researches the company specifically and names a real business problem they can help solve is remembered

The Positioning Checklist

  • Build a portfolio with 2–3 case studies showing your best work (Notion is free)
  • List the tools: Notion, Slack, Zoom, Asana, Google Workspace — even if it's from personal use
  • Rewrite your CV so every bullet has a result, not just a task
  • Apply to the role with a cover letter that names a specific challenge the company is facing

Apply to roles where you meet 70% of the requirements. That final 30% is what the interview, portfolio, and attitude are there to close.
