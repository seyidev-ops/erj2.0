How to Read a Remote Job Advert: What the Words Actually Mean

A job advert is a document written by a committee to protect itself. Read literally it wastes your evening; read properly it tells you the salary band, the real requirements, and whether you should apply at all.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 24 August 2026 6 min read

Most people read an advert to decide whether they are allowed to apply. That is the wrong question, and it is why so many good candidates disqualify themselves before anyone has seen them.

Read properly, an advert tells you what the job actually is, which requirements are real, and whether the company is worth an evening of your time.

"Remote" — establish which kind

The word covers at least four different arrangements. Search the full text before anything else.

Remote (Worldwide) or Remote (Global) — genuinely open. Remote (US), Remote (EMEA), Remote (UK) — a location requirement wearing the word remote. Remote-first — usually genuine, and usually a company that has thought about it. Hybrid or Remote with occasional travel — not remote for you.

Then find the sentence about time zones. "Must overlap 9–5 PST" is a different job from "4 hours overlap with CET", and one of those is comfortable from West Africa while the other means working until two in the morning.

The requirements list is two lists pretending to be one

Almost every advert mixes genuine requirements with a wish list assembled by adding everyone's preferences together. They are distinguishable.

Real requirements appear in the first two or three bullets, are repeated in the job summary at the top, and are specific — a named tool, a named certification, a legal right to work.

Wish-list items sit lower down, are hedged with "familiarity with", "exposure to", "a plus", "nice to have", and often contradict the seniority named in the title.

The practical rule: if you meet what is in the summary and the first three bullets, apply. Waiting until you meet all twelve means never applying, which has a known success rate.

Years of experience are a filter, not a wall

"5+ years" is generally a proxy for a level of independence, not a count. Someone with three years who can point at delivered work regularly beats someone with six who cannot.

What you cannot fake is the evidence. The number is negotiable; the artefact is not.

What the money section tells you

A published band is a good sign in itself — it usually means a company with a structured compensation process rather than one that pays whatever each candidate accepts.

"Competitive salary" means nothing. "Salary commensurate with experience and location" means there is a location-based band and you should ask about it early. No mention of money at all, in a market where publishing is increasingly normal, is worth noticing.

Read the responsibilities as a description of a day

Six bullets that all describe the same activity is a focused role. Twelve bullets spanning three different professions is a company trying to hire three people with one salary — sometimes a real opportunity early in a career, usually exhausting.

Watch for "wear many hats", "fast-paced environment", "self-starter with minimal supervision". None are disqualifying. Together, in a small company with no named manager for the role, they describe being left alone with a workload.

The tell at the bottom

Read the closing paragraph — the culture one everyone skips. Companies that have thought seriously about distributed work say concrete things there: how they document, how they handle time zones, what their meeting norms are.

Companies that have not say "we work hard and play hard" and mention a team retreat.

The practical use of all this

Decoding one advert properly takes about four minutes and saves the two hours you would have spent tailoring an application for a role that was never open to you.

It also gives you your tailoring material. The language the advert repeats is the language the screening software and the first human are both looking for — put those exact words, honestly, in your CV.
