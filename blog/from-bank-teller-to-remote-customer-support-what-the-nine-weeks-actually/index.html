From Bank Teller to Remote Customer Support: What the Nine Weeks Actually Looked Like

A composite drawn from several participants who made the same move. Real timeline, real setbacks, realistic numbers — including the months where nothing happened.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 11 July 2026 6 min read

This is a composite. The details come from several participants who made the same transition, combined into one timeline so the shape is visible. No single person had exactly this run, and that is the point — the shape repeats, the specifics never do.

The starting position

Four years as a bank teller in Ibadan. Take-home around 180,000 naira a month. Good with people, good under pressure, no international experience, no portfolio, and a CV that listed duties.

She had been applying to remote jobs for five months. Two replies, both rejections, one of them clearly automated.

Weeks one and two: stopping

The first thing she did was stop applying. That felt wrong and it was correct. Fifty more applications from the same CV would have produced the same nothing.

Instead she rewrote the CV around what she had actually done. Not "handled customer transactions" but "processed roughly 120 customer transactions a day with a error rate under one percent" and "trained four new tellers on the account-opening process".

Same four years. Completely different document.

Week three: the translation

The harder work was realising that banking-hall experience is customer support experience — she had just never described it that way. Complaint handling, de-escalation, working to a service standard, explaining a policy to someone who is angry about it.

She learned Zendesk basics from free documentation over one weekend, and added it honestly as "familiar with, not yet used professionally".

Weeks four to six: applying properly

Twelve applications a week, not sixty. Each one to a company that hired from Africa, each with the tools named in the listing reflected in her CV. She also sent six direct messages to support team leads on LinkedIn.

Nothing happened for two weeks. This is the part people are not warned about, and the part where most stop.

Week seven: the first call

A support role at a small European software company. It went badly. She was asked how she manages her time without a supervisor watching and had no real answer, because she had never had to.

She did not get it. She wrote down the four questions she had fumbled.

Weeks eight and nine: the offer

Two more interviews, both better because she had prepared the answers she failed the first time. The second produced an offer: a customer support role with a company running a distributed team, $1,100 a month to start, paid through Deel.

That is roughly four times her teller salary at the exchange rate at the time. It is also not $5,000, and it is not month one.

What actually mattered

  • Stopping the applications that were not working, instead of sending more of them.
  • Describing existing work in the buyer’s language rather than her employer’s.
  • Twelve careful applications beating sixty careless ones.
  • Treating the failed interview as a list of questions to prepare, not as a verdict.
  • Nine weeks. Not nine days.
