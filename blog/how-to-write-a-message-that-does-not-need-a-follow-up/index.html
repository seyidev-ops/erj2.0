How to Write a Message That Does Not Need a Follow-Up

In a distributed team, every unclear message costs a day. Here is the structure that gets a decision on the first reply, with before and after examples.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 21 July 2026 5 min read

When your colleague is six hours behind you, a message that raises a question instead of answering one costs a full day. Send three of those a week and you have lost most of a working week to clarification.

This is the single most valuable skill in remote work and almost nobody is taught it.

The structure

  1. What you need, in the first line. Decision, review, information, or nothing at all.
  2. The context — only what is needed to make that decision.
  3. Your recommendation. Never present a problem without your suggested answer.
  4. The deadline, with the time zone.

Before and after

Before: "Hi, quick question about the client report. Do you have a minute?"

This costs a reply, a wait, and then the actual question. Two days for something that should have taken one message.

After: "Decision needed by Thursday 5pm WAT. The client report is ready but the Q2 figures came in two days late, so I can either send on Thursday with a note that Q2 is provisional, or hold until Monday for final numbers. I recommend sending Thursday with the note, because they said they need something for their board on Friday. Say the word if you disagree."

One message. One reply. Done.

Four habits that do most of the work

Put the ask first. Not after three paragraphs of background. Busy people read the first line and decide whether to read the rest now or later.

Include the time zone, always. "By 5pm" is meaningless across four countries. "5pm WAT" is a fact.

Bring an option, not a question. "What should we do?" pushes work onto them. "I plan to do X unless you object" moves it forward and shows judgement.

Say what is not happening. If something slipped, say so directly, with the new date and the reason. Silence is read as a problem being hidden.

The end-of-day note

Four lines, sent before you close the laptop:

  • Done: what actually finished today
  • In progress: what is moving and when it lands
  • Blocked: what is stuck and who can unstick it
  • Need: what you need from someone, by when

This one habit does more for your reputation than almost anything else you can do. Your manager wakes up already knowing where things stand, never has to ask, and starts to trust you with bigger work. That is how remote promotions actually happen.

One thing to stop

Sending "hi" and waiting. Put the whole thing in one message. Every single time.
