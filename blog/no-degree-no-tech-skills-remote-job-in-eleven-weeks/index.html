No Degree, No Tech Skills, Remote Job in Eleven Weeks

A composite of participants who started from the furthest back. What she actually did, what it paid, and why the first role was worth taking anyway.

Oluwaseyi Ashiru 31 July 2026 6 min read

Composite, as with the others. This one matters because it starts from the position most people write off entirely.

The starting position

Thirty-four, secondary school certificate, no degree, no coding, no design. Six years running the front desk and back office of a small clinic in Enugu — booking patients, chasing suppliers, managing three staff rotas, handling angry people all day.

She thought she had no marketable skills. She had six years of operations experience and had never once called it that.

Week one: the renaming

The whole first week was translation. Not learning anything new — describing what already existed in language an international employer recognises.

  • "Booked appointments" became calendar and scheduling management for a team of nine
  • "Chased suppliers" became vendor coordination and procurement follow-up
  • "Handled complaints" became first-line customer resolution with escalation
  • "Did the rota" became rota planning and coverage management across three shifts

Nothing was invented. Every line was true. It had simply never been written in the buyer’s vocabulary.

Weeks two and three: the actual gaps

Two genuine gaps: she had never used the tools remote teams run on, and she had no shareable proof.

She spent two weeks on free versions of Google Workspace, Notion, Trello and Slack. Then she built three things: a full travel itinerary for a fictional three-city trip with costs and contingencies, an inbox management system written up as a document, and a Trello board for a clinic process she genuinely ran.

That third one was the strongest, because it was real.

Weeks four to seven: applying

Virtual assistant and operations support roles at small companies. Ten a week, plus messages to founders — because in companies under thirty people, the founder is usually the one hiring the assistant.

Two weeks of silence. Then a reply, then a rejection, then nothing again.

Week eight: the near miss

An interview with an American founder. It went well until he asked how she would handle a client emailing at 11pm her time. She said she would answer immediately. Wrong answer, and she knew it as she said it.

What he wanted to hear was that she would set expectations, define response windows, and protect a sustainable schedule — because a VA who burns out in three months is worse than no VA.

Week eleven

Offer: executive assistant to a founder at a small UK company. $850 a month, 25 hours a week, paid through Wise.

Not life-changing on its own. But it was more than her clinic salary for fewer hours, in a currency that does not lose value every quarter, and it made her someone with international experience.

What happened next matters more

Fourteen months later she was full-time with the same company at roughly $1,600, having taken over supplier management and basic bookkeeping.

The first role is rarely the destination. It is the thing that makes the second role possible, and the second role is usually where the number changes properly.

The point

She had no degree and no technical skill. What she had was six years of running things and no vocabulary for it. That is a translation problem, not a qualification problem, and it is far more common than people realise.
